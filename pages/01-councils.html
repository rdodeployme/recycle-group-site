01 — What we take

The difficult four, and everything around them.

Each of these needs its own machine, its own pathway and its own downstream buyer. That's the reason they usually get landfilled, and the reason we invested in them.

Mattresses & bases

12,000 to 30,000 a month

More than half of Victoria's discarded mattresses. Each one is dismantled in about 38 seconds — springs separated by magnets, vibration, high-volume suction and an electrostatic fibre separator; steel baled 99% clean and exported as a commodity; foam and fibre split off.

Soft furniture

Couches, chairs, bed bases

The other bulky item that defeats kerbside. Assessed for reuse first, then dismantled into timber, foam, fibre and metal through the same line as mattresses.

Polystyrene · Styro

300+ m³ a day

EPS is up to 98% air, so it is uneconomic to cart and wasteful to landfill. Five hot-melt units and one cold press densify it on site into solid blocks — three shipping containers of foam become a cube the size of a washing machine — and it is reprocessed into picture frames, skirting boards, wall panels and outdoor furniture.

Whitegoods, e-waste & cable

To AS/NZS 5377

Cable is granulated to separate copper from insulation. Electrical items and IT equipment are handled to AS/NZS 5377, with data-bearing devices sanitised to NIST 800-88.

Paint

Accepted at the transfer station — architectural and decorative paint goes down the Paintback pathway rather than into a landfill cell.

Green waste, soil & clean fill

Handled at the group's 400,000 m² Nunjara site at Moorabool: green waste into mulches and composts, concrete, brick and rock crushed into recycled aggregate, mixed soils into recycled blends.

The awkward leftovers

Car, domestic and lithium batteries, NOS canisters, fire extinguishers, tyres and printer cartridges — accepted, because the alternative is that they get dumped.

Not acceptedAsbestos, prescribed waste, hazardous chemicals, firearms or explosives. We would rather say no to those than pretend.

02 — Reuse first

The Mattress Project.

Between 5 and 10% of the mattresses collected each month are in good enough condition to be donated rather than dismantled. So before anything reaches the line, it gets looked at.

That is the order the whole group runs in — reuse, redistribute, recover, recycle, and landfill last. For a council it means a portion of what residents put on the nature strip goes to a household that needed it, and you have the numbers to say so.

04 — Where to start

One stream, one area, one month.

Nobody should sign a whole-of-council contract on the strength of a brochure. The practical way in is small and measurable.

  1. Come and see the floor

    An afternoon at North Geelong. Watch a mattress go through the line, see the streams in the bays, ask the operators.

  2. Pick the stream that hurts most

    Usually mattresses, sometimes polystyrene or soft furniture. Whatever is costing the most in landfill airspace.

  3. Agree the pathway and the paperwork

    Where it goes, who handles it, what gets reported and how often.

  4. Run one area, one round

    A pilot suburb or a single hard waste cycle, with real volumes rather than modelled ones.

  5. Read the numbers together

    What came in, what was reused, what was recovered, what was residual — and what it cost against the landfill alternative.

  6. Then decide how far it goes

    Extend the stream, add another, or stop. The pilot is designed so that stopping is a real option.

05 — Neighbouring councils

A region moves more than a council.

A single council's mattress volume rarely justifies a dedicated run. Several councils in the same region, going to the same facility on the same schedule, usually does — and the recovery rate, the reporting and the rate per unit all improve with it.

If your regional waste group is looking at a shared approach, that is the conversation we are best set up for. We are already presenting to forums on exactly this.
